# Question principles — what a teach-phase check must be

These govern `type: "check"` questions (and explain). Predictions have their own doctrine — the predict/check cycle file — and deliberately violate the first two principles: they are about the untaught next step, and guessing IS the point.

Every check exists to answer one question: did the idea actually **take** — not whether he can read it back. The principles:

- **Answerable by reasoning from the session.** Every check must be answerable by reasoning from what the session has covered — never require anything he couldn't have gotten from the lesson itself. A check he has no route to is not a check, it's a coin flip.
- **Never just reciting your words back.** A check that only asks him to parrot the sentence just above it is copywork, not evidence.
- **Prefer problems that apply the knowledge.** The strongest evidence that an idea took is seeing it USED — applied to a new case, used to predict something, extended somewhere it hasn't been. Reach for applied questions first; when the idea genuinely can't be applied, an honest recall check is fine — but it's the fallback, not the habit.
- **Catch confusion early, and make it nameable.** Design checks so a miss TELLS you which wrong model he holds — in a multiple-choice check each wrong option is the answer he’d give under a specific wrong model; in a free-recall check his own words reveal it. Diagnose and repair before building anything on top of it.
- **His answer is signal, not verdict.** A lucky right answer is nothing; a wrong answer on an honest check is gold — confusion caught early is cheap to fix.

## Options or his own words — choose per check

When both `quiz` (options) and `explain` (his own words, no options) are armed, the kind is part of the check's design:

- **`explain` when understanding shows in how he says it** — why something is true, what a term means in his words, how two ideas connect, what would change if one thing changed. And **when the answer is something to work out** — a number to compute, a sentence to build (in a language lesson: say it in the language), a short derivation. A computed answer is typed, not picked from four: picking only proves he can recognise it.
- **`quiz` when the point is to tell specific wrong models apart** — each wrong option the answer of one named misconception — or for a quick discrimination where writing would only slow him down.
- **Mix them — about one in three in his own words.** Recognition is not recall: a lesson of only options never shows that he can produce the idea himself, and never more than two option checks in a row when an `explain` would serve. But not every check either: writing (often by pen) costs him time, and options stay the quick check for a small step. Choose by what the step needs, not to hit a number.
- **Predictions can be either.** A free prediction — "what do you expect, and why?" — is often the stronger one, because his reason shows the model he is using.
- **Write an `explain` as one bounded ask.** One question, about a named case, with the shape of the answer in `details` — the craft is in the shared question-tools chunk ("Free-recall question craft"); an `explain` that reads as "tell me about X" is a miss before he has answered.
- **Grade an `explain` on substance.** Against your committed answer, credit the right idea in plain words; a missing piece is "partly", a wrong model is a miss — name which.
